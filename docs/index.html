What this won't tell you

It does not price anything, and that is deliberate. Everything above is arithmetic on your own meter readings, which is safe to compute for anyone. Turning it into dollars means modelling a specific tariff — and this project holds itself to a rule before any dollar figure ships:

No feature that produces a dollar figure ships until the bill engine reproduces real utility bills, from real interval data, within ±$2 per month.

That gate is met for PG&E: eleven consecutive real statements reproduced, worst error 22 cents. It is not yet met for SDG&E, because no real SDG&E bill has been tested against the engine. Rather than show you a number the engine hasn't earned, this tool shows you none. The methodology writeup explains how the bill engine works, what it still assumes, and where the remaining gaps are.

How to get your file

It is free, it takes a couple of minutes, and it is your data by law. You want the interval usage export — the hour-by-hour readings — not the billing or cost summary, which is a different download and will be rejected here.

PG&E
  1. Sign in at pge.com and open Energy Usage Details.
  2. Find the Green Button control and choose Export usage for a range of days.
  3. Pick the widest range offered (up to 13 months) and download the CSV.
SDG&E
  1. Sign in at sdge.com and open your usage / Green Button tool.
  2. Choose the interval usage export (15-minute or hourly readings).
  3. Pick the widest range offered (up to 13 months) and download the CSV.

If you have solar, your file will contain a separate export register. This tool reads it and will say so.

Why 13 months, and not less

California rates change between summer and winter, and so does most households' usage. A comparison built on three months of winter data will mis-rank plans for the summer you haven't measured. Both utilities keep 13 months available; export all of it while you can, because access usually ends when the account does.

Why trust the numbers

This tool runs the same Python that the project's test suite covers — the parsers are compiled to WebAssembly and executed in your browser, not reimplemented in JavaScript where the two could quietly drift apart. The bill engine behind the wider project is held to a published accuracy record against real statements.
